\begin{abstract}
Surveillance footage is often affected by authentic camera distortions, such as
defocus blur and exposure error, that are rarely represented in activity-recognition
benchmarks. The Authentically Distorted Surveillance Videos (AD-SVD) dataset already
carries activity and imaging-condition annotations and has been used for tracking,
performance prediction and motion classification. We use these annotations as labels for
eight-way activity classification and four-way imaging-condition classification (pristine,
exposure, focus, exposure+focus), and evaluate seven traditional classifiers, including a
multilayer perceptron, on handcrafted features computed from foreground-detection and
tracking output. We test the hypothesis that, under authentic distortion, the quality of
foreground detection limits accuracy more than the choice of classifier. A fixed-threshold

\section{Introduction}

The growth of public surveillance infrastructure has created a 
fundamental mismatch between the volume of video produced and 
the capacity of human operators to monitor it effectively. 
Sustained monitoring of many camera feeds is a vigilance task, and human 
detection performance on such tasks degrades with time on task~\cite{warm2008vigilance,donald2015classification}, 
while the number of cameras keeps growing~\cite{tsakanikas2017video}. Automated activity 
recognition is therefore a core capability of intelligent surveillance systems, although 
it is not the only motivation: automated analysis also supports forensic search, 
retrospective review and the triage of alerts for human operators. 
\todo{confirm the three vigilance/CCTV citations against the publisher pages; they replace Ahmed et al., which does not support this claim}

Although deep learning has achieved impressive performance on large-scale 
action-recognition benchmarks~\cite{ur2023use,xiao2016overview}, its dependence 
on extensive labelled datasets, high computational requirements, and limited 
interpretability continue to present practical challenges for resource-constrained 
deployments~\cite{csurka2018handcrafted,arias2018deep}. Classical handcrafted 
representations remain attractive because they provide transparency, computational 
efficiency, and strong performance in data-constrained settings, but their 
effectiveness depends critically on reliable foreground extraction, making 
them vulnerable to authentic camera distortions such as defocus blur and exposure 
variation, which degrade the edge contrast and luminance information required by 
background subtraction algorithms~\cite{gillani2025distortion}.

Most existing activity-recognition benchmarks are collected under comparatively 
clean recording conditions and therefore provide limited insight into how authentic, 
in-capture distortions affect downstream recognition performance. 
The Authentically Distorted Surveillance Videos (AD-SVD) dataset 
addresses this gap. It was introduced for visual object tracking and quality 
assessment~\cite{gomeznieto2019how,9698081}, and carries activity and imaging-condition 
annotations. Prior use has been at the level of trackers and quality prediction: 
Gomez-Nieto et al.~\cite{9698081} benchmark ten trackers and predict a tracker's AUC 
with a regression model; Gomez-Nieto's thesis~\cite{gomeznieto2021thesis} trains a 
CNN to separate pristine from exposure-distorted frames; and two recent studies use 
detection-and-tracking pipelines on AD-SVD clips and report human-motion classification 
for walking, running and prowling~\cite{shaban2024abnormal,shaban2025multicamera}. To our 
knowledge, none of these evaluates traditional machine-learning classifiers on the full 
set of eight activity classes and four imaging conditions, nor asks which pipeline stage 
limits accuracy. We fill that narrower gap. Baselines of this kind let future methods be 
compared under realistic deployment conditions rather than idealised laboratory 
environments.

We therefore use the existing AD-SVD annotations as labels for two supervised tasks, 
activity classification and imaging-condition classification, and investigate whether 
handcrafted trajectory representations retain sufficient discriminative information 
for reliable activity recognition after authentic distortion has degraded foreground 
detection, and whether the same motion representations also encode enough information 
to distinguish distortion types without requiring explicit image-quality analysis. 
Underlying both questions is a working hypothesis: that under authentic distortion, 
the quality of foreground detection, not the choice of classifier or the richness 
of the feature set, sets the ceiling on downstream classification accuracy. The 
remainder of the paper tests this hypothesis directly.

The contributions of this paper are: (i) an evaluation protocol for activity and 
imaging-condition classification on AD-SVD, with fixed cross-validation that keeps 
near-duplicate recordings together, macro-averaged metrics and bootstrap intervals; 
(ii) a comparison of seven traditional classifiers (Random Forest, a from-scratch Random 
Forest, SVM, KNN, LightGBM, XGBoost and an MLP) under the same pipelines; 
(iii) a controlled $2{\times}2{\times}2$ factorial ablation of detector, preprocessing 
and feature set, together with a feature-family ablation, to test the hypothesis above; 
(iv) a confidence-driven adaptive MOG2 detector, with a sensitivity analysis of its 
hyperparameters and a comparison against ViBe, PBAS and SuBSENSE \todo{results}; and 
(v) a complete feature specification and released code, so the benchmark can be reproduced.

The rest of the paper follows this plan. Section~2 reviews related work. Section~3 
describes the proposed classification pipeline, including the adaptive detection 
component. Section~4 presents the experimental design and research questions. 
Section~5 answers each research question in turn and discusses implications. 
Section~6 concludes and outlines future work.

\section{Related Work}

Conventional activity recognition predates deep learning and follows a recognisable 
sequence~\cite{aggarwal2011human,poppe2010survey}. Early systems segment moving 
foreground with background subtraction~\cite{stauffer1999adaptive,zivkovic2004improved}, 
track it, and describe motion with silhouette-based templates such as motion-history 
images~\cite{bobick2002recognition}, with trajectory statistics~\cite{ahmed2018trajectory}, 
or with local spatio-temporal descriptors such as space-time interest 
points~\cite{laptev2005space} and dense and improved 
trajectories~\cite{wang2012dense,wang2013improved}; a classifier such as an SVM or a 
random forest then maps the descriptor to an activity label. Their documented limits are 
that silhouette- and trajectory-based descriptors depend on reliable foreground 
extraction, that hand-designed descriptors transfer poorly across scenes, and that 
accuracy saturates below that of learned representations when training data are 
plentiful~\cite{ur2023use,xiao2016overview}. These methods 
remain attractive because they are computationally efficient, interpretable, and require 
substantially less labelled data than modern deep learning approaches, and trajectory-based 
representations in particular have demonstrated competitive performance for surveillance 
applications by effectively capturing human motion patterns while remaining suitable for 
real-time deployment in resource-constrained environments~\cite{ahmed2018trajectory,csurka2018handcrafted}. 
Recent advances in deep learning have significantly improved activity recognition through 
convolutional, recurrent, and transformer-based architectures that learn rich spatio-temporal 
representations directly from video data, achieving state-of-the-art performance on large-scale 
benchmarks, such as UCF101, HMDB51, Kinetics, and Something-Something~\cite{ur2023use,xiao2016overview}. 
Their success, however, generally depends on extensive annotated datasets, considerable computational 
resources, and carefully curated training data, which present practical challenges for surveillance 
applications where labelled data are often limited and deployment constraints favour computationally 
efficient solutions. Consequently, traditional machine learning combined with handcrafted feature 
representations continues to provide a strong and practical baseline for surveillance scenarios 
with limited training data.

Although considerable progress has been made in activity recognition, relatively little attention 
has been devoted to understanding the impact of authentic camera distortions on downstream 
classification performance. Most existing benchmarks either introduce synthetic degradations 
or are collected under favourable recording conditions, limiting their ability to represent 
the challenges encountered by real-world surveillance systems; authentic distortions, 
including defocus blur and exposure variation, degrade foreground quality, fragment object 
trajectories, and consequently reduce the reliability of motion-based feature 
extraction~\cite{gillani2025distortion}. What remains unanswered in this literature is which 
stage of the pipeline actually absorbs that damage: whether the loss is better characterised 
as a classifier-capacity problem, a feature-representation problem, or a detection problem 
upstream of both. Adaptive background-subtraction methods proposed for challenging conditions include the 
Visual Background Extractor (ViBe)~\cite{barnich2011vibe}, the Pixel-Based Adaptive 
Segmenter (PBAS)~\cite{hofmann2012background} and the Self-Balanced Sensitivity Segmenter 
(SuBSENSE)~\cite{stcharles2015subsense}. We build on MOG2~\cite{zivkovic2004improved,zivkovic2006efficient} 
because it is lightweight and widely available, and compare the result against ViBe, PBAS and 
SuBSENSE under the same tracking, features and classifiers (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}).

Gomez-Nieto et al.~\cite{9698081} introduced AD-SVD to evaluate object tracking and 
tracker-performance prediction under authentic camera distortions. Later uses stay close to 
that setting: the thesis~\cite{gomeznieto2021thesis} classifies pristine versus 
exposure-distorted frames with a CNN, and Shaban et al.~\cite{shaban2024abnormal,shaban2025multicamera} 
report human-motion classification for walking, running and prowling from detector-and-tracker 
output. What remains open is whether the detection difficulties that complicate tracking also 
limit supervised classification over all eight activities and all four imaging conditions, 
and whether classifier choice can compensate for them.

\section{Proposed Classification Pipeline}

This section describes the pipeline for transforming raw, authentically distorted 
surveillance videos into activity and distortion predictions. The pipeline is 
formulated as a sequential information transformation, illustrated in 
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}:
\[
V \;\xrightarrow{f_{\text{pre}}}\; V'
  \;\xrightarrow{f_{\text{det}}}\; M
  \;\xrightarrow{f_{\text{trk}}}\; \mathcal{T}
  \;\xrightarrow{f_{\text{feat}}}\; \mathbf{x}
  \;\xrightarrow{f_{\text{clf}}}\; (\hat{a},\,\hat{c}),
\]
where $V'$ denotes the preprocessed video, $M$ the foreground-mask sequence, 
$\mathcal{T}$ the set of valid object trajectories, $\mathbf{x}$ the aggregated 
video-level feature vector, $\hat{a}$ the predicted activity class, and $\hat{c}$ 
the predicted imaging condition. Because information discarded at one stage cannot 
be recovered later, the pipeline is deliberately structured to test the hypothesis 
raised in Section~1: if foreground detection is the weakest stage, then strengthening 
it, rather than enriching preprocessing, features, or classifier choice downstream, 
should account for most of the achievable gain. Section~3 introduces adaptive 
detection as the mechanism for doing so; Sections~4 and~5 isolate its 
contribution from the other stages.

\paragraph{Distortion-aware preprocessing.}

Each video frame is resized to $640\times480$ to provide a consistent 
input resolution while reducing computational cost. Blur is identified 
using the variance of the Laplacian (threshold 100), after which an 
unsharp mask ($\sigma=2.0$, strength 1.5) enhances edge contrast. 
Exposure abnormalities are detected from image intensity statistics 
(lower and upper thresholds of 60 and 195, respectively) and corrected 
using Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) with a 
clip limit of 2.0 and an $8\times8$ tile grid. These operations 
improve local contrast and edge definition before foreground 
extraction while preserving the natural characteristics of the 
recorded distortions.

\paragraph{Confidence-driven adaptive foreground detection.}

Foreground segmentation is performed using MOG2~\cite{zivkovic2004improved,zivkovic2006efficient} 
with a history length of 20, a nominal variance threshold $\tau$ 
\todo{the first submission used $\tau=25$; the code default is now 16 (the OpenCV/Zivkovic value); choose with mog2\_threshold\_sweep.py, then re-run every table at that single value}, 
and shadow detection disabled. Each frame's mask is scored by a mask-quality function 
$Q_{\text{mask}} \in [0,1]$ that combines three measures multiplicatively, 
so that a failure on any single axis collapses the overall score:
\begin{equation}
Q_{\text{mask}} = C \cdot F \cdot S,
\label{eq:maskquality}
\end{equation}
where $C$ is a coverage score peaked around 2\% foreground area, $F$ is a 
fragmentation score that decays linearly once the blob count exceeds 30, 
and $S$ is a resolution-invariant blob-size score. This mask score is 
then combined with an exponential-moving-average track-quality prior 
$Q_{\text{track}}$, updated once per frame from the previous frame's 
tracker output, to give the overall confidence:
\begin{equation}
Q = w \, Q_{\text{mask}} + (1-w) \, Q_{\text{track}}, \qquad w = 0.7.
\label{eq:confidence}
\end{equation}
If $Q$ falls below a threshold of 0.4, detection is re-run at a relaxed 
variance threshold interpolated between $\tau$ and a relaxed target $\tau_r=10$ in 
proportion to how far below $q=0.4$ the confidence falls, clipped to a floor 
$\tau_{\min}=8$ (because $Q\ge 0$, the relaxed threshold never falls below $\tau_r$, so a floor 
at or below $\tau_r$ has no effect), and the re-run mask is kept only if its mask-quality score 
improves on the original (Algorithm~\ref{alg:adaptive}). Morphological 
closing (two iterations) followed by opening (one iteration) removes 
small gaps and isolated noise, while connected components smaller than 
500 pixels are discarded. The background model is updated once per frame, by the nominal 
call; the relaxed pass re-segments the same frame with learning rate zero. The first 
submission applied a second learning update on relaxed frames; all results in this version 
use the single-update rule. The first ten frames (half the history length) are never adapted, to let the model warm up. 
Equation~\ref{eq:maskquality} is a design choice, not a fitted quantity: we check it against 
ground-truth boxes in Section~\ref{sec:ablations} \todo{Spearman correlation between $Q_{\text{mask}}$ and 
F-measure against ground truth, from validate\_mask\_quality.py}. For brevity we refer to this component as 
Adaptive MOG2 throughout the rest of the paper.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\small
\caption{Confidence-driven Adaptive MOG2 (per frame)}
\label{alg:adaptive}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require frame $I$, nominal threshold $\tau$, relaxed target $\tau_r=10$, floor $\tau_{\min}=8$, confidence threshold $q=0.4$
\State $M \gets \textsc{MOG2}(I, \tau)$; $Q_{\text{mask}} \gets$ score of $M$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:maskquality})
\State $Q \gets w\,Q_{\text{mask}} + (1-w)\,Q_{\text{track}}$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:confidence})
\If{$Q \geq q$}
    \State \Return detections from $M$ \Comment{confident — keep}
\Else
    \State $\alpha \gets 1 - Q / q$; \; $\tau' \gets \tau - \alpha(\tau - \tau_r)$, clipped to $[\tau_{\min}, \tau]$
    \State $M' \gets \textsc{MOG2}(I, \tau')$; \; $Q'_{\text{mask}} \gets$ score of $M'$
    \State \Return detections from $M'$ if $Q'_{\text{mask}} > Q_{\text{mask}}$, else from $M$
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Tracking.}

Detected foreground regions are associated across consecutive frames using 
IoU-based matching (threshold 0.3) solved with the Hungarian algorithm; 
tracks may disappear for up to 30 frames before termination, and trajectories 
are capped at 300 frames. No track is removed after tracking in the main configuration: 
the trajectory, circular-motion, kinematic and shape descriptors use every track with at least 
two to four points, and the wavelet and interaction descriptors skip tracks shorter than 15 frames. 
A stricter variant that discards tracks shorter than 30 frames before feature extraction is evaluated 
separately \todo{group\_i\_minlen30\_filtered result; decide which variant is the main one}. 
Each track records the frame index of every point, and the interaction descriptors compare two 
tracks only on the frames in which both were observed.

\paragraph{Feature families.}

We use \emph{descriptor} for a quantity computed from one track and \emph{feature} for an entry of the 
video-level vector given to a classifier. Every descriptor is listed in Appendix~\ref{app:features}. Six 
descriptor families (trajectory, circular-motion, kinematic, shape, wavelet and interaction; 45 descriptors 
per track) are pooled across a video's tracks by their mean, maximum and standard deviation (135 features) 
and joined by the track count, eight temporal-context scalars and three file-metadata scalars (frame count, 
duration, frame rate), giving 147 features. The proposed configuration adds four video-level families that 
do \emph{not} depend on tracking: 16 image-quality features (Laplacian, Tenengrad and Brenner sharpness and 
brightness statistics), 22 global optical-flow features, 34 appearance features (uniform local binary 
patterns and a six-bin gradient-orientation histogram, not the full HOG descriptor), and two track-presence 
indicators, giving 221 features. The trajectory-only baseline has 54 (14 trajectory descriptors pooled three 
ways, plus the track count, temporal context and metadata). Videos for which a feature is undefined (for 
example, no track long enough for the wavelet descriptor) receive the value zero for it.

\paragraph{Classification and evaluation protocol.}

Feature vectors are standardised inside a scikit-learn \texttt{Pipeline}, so scaling is fitted on 
training folds only. Seven classifiers are evaluated in every configuration with fixed 
hyperparameters (no tuning, hence no nested validation): Random Forest (200 trees, balanced class 
weights), a from-scratch Random Forest (200 trees, depth 10, information-gain splits), SVM (RBF kernel, 
$C=10$, $\gamma$ = \texttt{scale}, balanced class weights, scikit-learn's one-vs-one \texttt{SVC}), 
KNN ($k=5$, Euclidean), LightGBM (200 estimators, depth 6, balanced class weights), 
XGBoost (200 estimators, depth 6, no class weights) and an MLP (two hidden layers of 128 and 64 units, 
$L_2$ penalty $10^{-3}$, early stopping, balanced sample weights). The tree ensembles are included because 
they handle mixed-scale, partly redundant tabular features without tuning; SVM and KNN represent 
margin- and distance-based learners; the MLP is a neural counterpart on the identical feature vectors. 
There is no separate test set: each classifier is scored by out-of-fold predictions from stratified 
five-fold cross-validation (about 80\% of videos train and 20\% test in each fold), repeated with 
\todo{3--5} different shuffles, and all classifiers see identical folds. Because AD-SVD includes 
re-encoded and multi-level versions of recordings~\cite{akyk-7b78-20}, folds are built so that videos of the 
same recording are never split between training and test \todo{describe the grouping used (audit\_duplicates.py), 
its group-size histogram, and the ungrouped result for comparison}. The primary metric is macro-averaged F1, 
reported with balanced accuracy and accuracy; 95\% intervals are bootstrap percentile intervals over videos. 
When a table reports ``the best classifier'', that classifier was chosen on the same folds it is scored on, 
so its score is mildly optimistic; we therefore also report all classifiers.

\paragraph{Baseline configuration.}

To quantify the influence of foreground detection on downstream performance, 
a fixed-threshold baseline is evaluated using standard preprocessing, 
conventional MOG2 with a fixed variance threshold \todo{same $\tau$ as the proposed configuration}, 
IoU-Hungarian tracking, and a 54-dimensional trajectory-only feature set, 
intentionally conservative rather than artificially weakened. 
The from-scratch Random Forest (information-gain splitting, bootstrap aggregation) 
is included to compare a first-principles implementation against established libraries.

\paragraph{Factorial ablation.}

The first submission compared three configurations that differed in several factors at once, so a gain 
could not be assigned to the detector. We now cross three binary factors: detector (fixed versus adaptive 
MOG2), preprocessing (standard versus distortion-aware) and feature set (54 trajectory-only versus 221 
multi-family), giving eight cells, all run with the same classifiers and folds. The effect of the detector 
is then read as the difference between two cells that differ only in the detector, at each setting of the 
other two factors, with bootstrap intervals over the same resampled videos. 
A further set of runs removes feature families: without image-quality and metadata features, and with 
trajectory-derived features only. Alternative adaptive background models (ViBe, PBAS, SuBSENSE) are 
run through the same tracking, features and classifiers.

\paragraph{Why deep video models are not the primary comparison.}

AD-SVD has 4,476 videos in eight activity classes. End-to-end video networks are usually trained on 
far more: UCF101 has 13,320 clips in 101 classes~\cite{soomro2012ucf101}, and strong results on such 
benchmarks come from pre-training on Kinetics-scale data~\cite{carreira2017quo}. We do not claim that 
deep models would fail on AD-SVD, only that a from-scratch comparison would not be a fair test at this 
size and that a pre-trained one tests transfer rather than the pipeline studied here. The classifier-level 
neural comparison we can make cleanly is an MLP on the same handcrafted features, which is included. 
A head-to-head with a fine-tuned pre-trained video model is future work.

\section{Experimental Design}

This section describes the AD-SVD dataset, the experimental
configurations evaluated in this study, the evaluation protocol, and the
research questions addressed in Section~5.

The AD-SVD dataset~\cite{9698081,akyk-7b78-20} contains 4,476 surveillance
videos spanning eight suspicious activity classes recorded under four
imaging conditions (Pristine, Exposure, Focus, and Exposure+Focus); the distorted
conditions come at several severity levels and were exported at several H.264 qualities.
Both labels are taken from the dataset's own annotations (\texttt{datasetInfo.xlsx}). The videos were captured using four surveillance cameras
across seven locations, providing realistic variations in viewpoint,
scene layout, and imaging conditions. Two yields are reported for every configuration and must not be confused: the
\emph{feature yield}, the share of videos that produce a feature vector, and the
\emph{trajectory yield}, the share that keep at least one track. In the trajectory-only
configurations the two coincide, because a video without a track produces no vector and
is excluded. In the multi-family configurations they do not: the image-quality, optical-flow
and appearance features exist without any track, so such a video is kept with zero-valued
trajectory features. Section~\ref{sec:results} therefore reports trajectory yield, and
scores that depend on the set of videos kept are also reported on the videos common to all
configurations.

All experiments use the protocol of Section~3 (stratified, group-aware five-fold
cross-validation with identical folds across classifiers). Because the imaging-condition
classes are heavily imbalanced (160 Pristine videos against about 1,450 in each other
condition), accuracy alone can reward a model that ignores Pristine. The primary metric is
therefore macro-averaged F1~\cite{sokolova2009systematic}, reported with balanced
accuracy~\cite{brodersen2010balanced}, accuracy, and per-class precision and recall.
Uncertainty is shown as bootstrap percentile intervals over
videos~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}. We do not use paired $t$-tests over the five folds: the folds
share training data and are not independent, so such tests overstate significance
\cite{dietterich1998approximate,nadeau2003inference}. Differences between configurations are
instead reported as paired bootstrap differences over the same videos, and between classifiers
with McNemar's test on out-of-fold predictions.

\paragraph{Research Questions.}

The evaluation is organised around four research questions, each addressed by a subsection of
Section~5.
\textbf{RQ1}: does adaptive foreground detection recover trajectories from more videos than a fixed
threshold (trajectory yield)?
\textbf{RQ2}: when the other factors are held fixed, does the detector change classification
performance, and by how much compared with preprocessing, features and classifier?
\textbf{RQ3}: how do the seven classifiers compare under the baseline and the proposed pipeline?
\textbf{RQ4}: how much of the imaging-condition result depends on motion statistics, as opposed to
features that measure blur and exposure directly?

\section{Conclusion}

We evaluated activity and imaging-condition classification on AD-SVD with seven traditional classifiers on
handcrafted features, and tested whether foreground detection limits accuracy more than the classifier does.
\todo{write the conclusion from the regenerated factorial results: state the detector effect with its interval,
the trajectory yield of fixed versus adaptive MOG2, how much of the imaging-condition result survives without
image-quality and metadata features, and whether the gain is specific to adaptive MOG2 or shared by ViBe, PBAS and
SuBSENSE}. The first submission's headline numbers (46.5\% to 99.7\% yield, 75.5\% activity, 97.7\%
imaging-condition accuracy) should not be quoted until they have been reproduced with grouped folds, a single
model update, a trajectory-based yield and one consistent $\tau$.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Results come from one dataset, with cross-validation rather than a held-out test set, and with fixed
hyperparameters. Folds are grouped by recording only as well as the duplicate audit can identify recordings;
residual near-duplicates would still inflate scores. Adaptive MOG2 was evaluated only on AD-SVD. The
mask-quality function is a heuristic whose agreement with ground truth is measured against boxes, not silhouettes.
The interaction descriptors assume tracks that overlap in time, and the mean-direction descriptor averages angles
linearly, which is wrong near the 0/360$^\circ$ wrap; the circular-motion family supplies the correct statistic. Adaptive
MOG2 adds a second segmentation pass on low-confidence frames. The case against deep models rests on dataset size and
on the MLP comparison, not on a trained end-to-end video model.

\paragraph{Future work.}
Natural next steps are a fine-tuned pre-trained video model on the same folds, stronger tracking (for example
TFCR~\cite{yuan2020learning}), evaluation on other authentically distorted datasets, and an end-to-end learned
detector that needs no threshold.
